    Reading a poll like a pollster

    • Check the . A party on 5.5% in a poll of 1,000 could really be anywhere from about 4% to 7%.
    • Polls measure the party vote only. They do not tell you who wins each electorate, which is why different news outlets turn the same poll into different seat counts.
    • Watch the undecided. In the late-September 1News–Verian poll, 13% of people did not know or would not say.
    • Compare like with like. Follow one pollster over time, or the average of all of them.

    Your views: what if everyone thought like you?

    Here are 30 statements about how New Zealand should be run. Say how much you agree or disagree with each one. A last, optional step asks how much you trust each party leader. The site compares your answers with where it has placed seven parties, then works out the share of the each party might get if the whole country had views like yours. When you finish, the site draws the Parliament that would make.

    Your answers stay on this device and are not sent to anyone. This is a thinking tool. It does not tell you how to vote, and it is not a prediction.

    How the percentages are worked out

    For each statement, every party gets points for how close it sits to your answer.

    Each party’s points are averaged across the statements you answered. National’s and Labour’s points are then doubled. In real elections the two biggest parties win far more votes than people’s views alone would suggest, partly because many people vote for a party that could lead the government. Doubling makes the percentages look more like a real election. If you rated the leaders, each rating then moves that party’s points up or down a little: 20% up for “A lot”, 20% down for “Not at all”, and no change for “Don’t know”. Then the parties share out 100% in proportion to their points. Two parties on the same step get the same points, so they get the same share. A party placed “in between” gets points halfway between the two steps. If a party has no clear position on a statement, that statement is left out for that party.

    Why your result is not a prediction. Real voters also think about leaders, trust, their local area and how they have voted before. Here, every party that is near you gets some of the vote, so results are more even than a real election. A different set of 30 statements would give different numbers.

      How the Māori electorates work

      There are seven Māori electorates. They cover the whole country and sit on top of the general electorates like a second map.

      Voters of Māori descent choose whether to be on the or the general roll. Everyone gets the same party vote, whichever roll they are on.

      Why these seats can cause an . Many Māori roll voters . In 2023 Te Pāti Māori won six of these seats, but its party vote only earned four. It kept all six, so Parliament grew to 122.

      Read more about the Māori electorates

      Parliament has had Māori electorates since 1867. There were four for more than a century. Since MMP began, the number has depended on how many voters choose the Māori roll, and there have been seven since 2002.

      You can switch rolls at any time except in the three months before a general election. In July 2026, 302,096 Māori voters were on the Māori roll and 255,627 were on the general roll.

      Te Tai Tonga is the biggest electorate in the country. It covers the South Island, Stewart Island, the Chatham Islands and part of Wellington.

      This year Te Pāti Māori is asking Māori roll voters to split their votes, and Labour is standing in all seven seats. NZ First wants a public vote on whether to keep the Māori electorates, while other parties want to keep them.
